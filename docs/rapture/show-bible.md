# PROJECT: LET THE RAPTURES COMMENCE — 8-part British black comedy

## Premise

A fourteen-year-old called Max, making a retro poster for his esports team The Raptors, misspells the name as RAPTURES on an unlabelled 1980s terminal his dad bought in a job lot of a dead rockstar's belongings. The machine accepts it, and the tagline "LET THE RAPTURES COMMENCE", and asks for parameters. Max reads them as character stats and arranges them to look nice on the card, then asks it to clear his team's past attempts. Four billion people are sorted by criteria a child laid out for visual balance. Nobody on earth or in the afterlife can find the pattern, because there isn't one.

## Tone

Black comedy. Dry, deadpan, English. Douglas Adams and Dirk Gently for the bureaucracy; the cops are stupid in the manner of *Dirk Gently* or *Common Side Effects* — loose dialogue, wrong details noticed at length, long pauses, no punchlines. Nobody in the series knows they're in an apocalypse; they know they're in a *situation*, and each has translated the end of the world into the terms they already used. The grim material is texture, never pathos. Never play a beat for sadness. No quiet melancholy inserts, no reflective endings, no grief. Nothing dramatic ever happens dramatically: the rapture, the violence and the correction all occur with no flash, no sound and no score, and the camera keeps operating as though the subject has merely walked out of frame.

## Cast

- **Nina Doyle, 45.** Nun, runs St Jude's, a residential house for people the system gave up on. Stubborn, self-sufficient, doesn't accept help, lies fluently to authority, can re-plumb a building. Daughter of the 1980 rockstar; never knew him. Half-angel, therefore ineligible for sorting. Treats divine instruction as an unreliable supplier sending vague orders. Complains upward. Has her mother's pendant.
- **PC Kath Bridges (white, 30s)** and **PC Ray Okonjo (black, 30s).** Sincerely believe they were left behind to teach moral justice, can't remember who told them that. Terrible officers. Maintain paperwork religiously. Cite superiors who no longer exist.
- **Danny, 40, and Jodie, 11.** Scavenging for water. He has rules; she's better at crime than he is and knows it. Handheld, dark, red.
- **Martin, 40s.** Failed musician, denim jacket correct in 1994, four guitars, nothing written in eleven years. Spent his life collecting the rockstar's belongings. Owns the machine and thinks it's junk. Can't reach his son.
- **Brenda and Gordon.** Limbo guides. Bored, sincere, customer-service warmth. No records on anyone.
- **Vassago, Reek, Tamsin.** Hell's intake. Thick. Decided Heaven stole their queue and went to catch them at it. Investigating entirely the wrong thing all season.
- **Pat, 70s.** A demon doing an audit disguised as an old lady. Overcommitted disguise. Husband's name changes.
- **Alan, 50s.** A blank. Bag for life containing another bag.

## The blanks (NPCs)

Always existed — accountants, the ones who bore you to death, men who do everything their wife tells them. Some began glitching after the rapture and are used by Heaven and Hell as cameras. Their tell is the phrase "I can only apologise" — a complete response with nothing behind it, no excuse, no defensiveness, nothing to argue with. Half of real humanity says it too, which makes every test for them useless. Nicknames vary by faction: extras, blanks, monos, greycoats.

## Cosmology

No God, no AI god — just separate universes for each religion and an administration between them. Limbo is a cathedral: stone, vaulting, stained glass a hundred feet up, and the admin bolted into it — rope barriers screwed into marble, a ticket dispenser cable-tied to a pillar, folding tables with laminated signs. Packed and everyone irritated, absorbed in the queue rather than the fact of being dead. Hell is a corporate open-plan floor with a blank target board and somebody's deflating birthday balloons. Nobody meets God; nobody can get one on the phone.

## Season clock

The water. Nobody's left who knows how to run a pump. Fine in ep1, brown by ep3, upstairs taps dead by ep5, dry by ep6. The apocalypse as a maintenance backlog.

## Visual grammar, one per thread — never mix them

- Nina: locked off, wide, deep focus, daylight, symmetrical. Camera never follows her. Long lenses on the road.
- Cops: static two-shot through the windscreen. Sitcom frame. Handheld only when they commit violence, then straight back to the identical framing as if nothing happened.
- Danny & Jodie: handheld, tight, dark, red practical sources only.
- Limbo: frontal, proscenium, vertical compositions, crowded. Python staging.
- Hell/demons: surveillance. Fixed high-corner cameras, 4:3, timecode, never moves, never close.
- Martin: fluorescent, flat, framings slightly off-centre.
- 1980: warm, grainy, anamorphic, practical sources through net curtains.
- Episode eight, the arcade: all grammars collapse. Neutral, unstyled.

## Output format

Numbered shots. Each shot: a slug for the type of shot, lens, and how the camera is mounted, then a short plain description. Dialogue indented beneath the shot it falls in. Mark pauses in seconds where the pause is the joke. No scene-setting prose, no camera poetry, no explanation of subtext.
